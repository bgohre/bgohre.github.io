The administrator's seat

The job description that never matches the job

Program administrators carry expertise that rarely makes it onto paper. Naming it is the first step toward real partnership.

BG Barbara GohreSeptember 2026 · 2 min read

Ask ten program administrators to compare their job descriptions and you'll likely get ten different documents. Ask them what they actually do all day, and the lists start to look remarkably alike.

They track accreditation requirements across every part of a program. They manage the systems that hold trainee data, from ADS to evaluation platforms to duty hour reports. They know which evaluations are overdue, where the documentation gaps live, and what each program letter of agreement says. They keep committees running. They're often the first person a struggling trainee talks to.

Very little of that shows up in a formal description. The gap matters more than it seems.

Why the gap matters

When a role isn't clearly defined, its work is easy to overlook. Good work turns invisible precisely because nothing went wrong. Administrators end up carrying responsibility without the authority, time, or recognition that should come with it.

It also shapes how people enter the field. A new administrator handed a vague description has to learn the real job by trial and error. That's one reason I care so much about onboarding.

Partnership, not support

I don't see the program administrator as a helper to the program director. I see two kinds of expertise working side by side. Physician leaders bring clinical and educational judgment. Administrators bring deep knowledge of accreditation, operations, and the systems that hold a program together.

The strongest programs don't treat the administrator as support. They treat the role as a partner with its own expertise.

Where to start

You don't have to wait for a national standard to make your role more visible. A few steps help:

  • Write down what you actually do. Keep a running list for one month. Most people are surprised by its length.
  • Name the expertise, not only the tasks. "Maintains ADS" is a task. "Monitors accreditation data and flags risk before annual review" describes judgment.
  • Use the list. Bring it to your next conversation about your role, your review, or your workload.
  • Ask for a defined seat. When your work is being discussed, ask to be in the room with a clear role there.

The role has grown a great deal over the past two decades. The way we describe it should grow too.

I'd like to hear how your role compares. Tell me what your job description leaves out.
